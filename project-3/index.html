1.2: Noising Process

1.2.1: Training

After one epoch the digits are already recovered but dim and smudged. By epoch 5 they are bright and clean.

1.2.2: Out-of-Distribution Testing

Up to σ = 0.6 it still works well. At 0.8 and 1.0 it starts turning noise into strokes: the 7 starts to look like a Z, and the 1 grows a crossbar.

1.2.3: Denoising Pure Noise

Patterns: every input gives the same blurry blob, a ghostly mix of 0, 3 and 8, nearly identical to the mean of all training digits above. It isn't any one of the classes 0 to 9 but an overlay of all of them. The loss flattens out at about 0.07 within the first ~100 steps and never improves.

Why: the noise carries no information about which digit is the target, so the best the network can do is give the same answer every time. Under MSE, the single output that minimizes the average squared error to all training digits is their mean. So the network learns to ignore its input and output the average digit.

After one epoch most samples are already the requested digit, but rough (the 4s and 8s are barely readable). By epoch 5 they are clean, and epoch 10 is about the same. This is much better than the time-only model, since the model no longer has to decide which digit to draw.

Without the learning rate scheduler.

To make up for removing the decay, I used a constant learning rate of 10−3 instead of 10−2, which is the rate the schedule decays to by the end.

Performance held up. After 10 epochs the samples look as good as with the scheduler, and after epoch 1 they are actually cleaner, since 10−3 is a steadier starting rate than 10−2. The loss curve is a bit noisier at the end, since the learning rate never decreases.

What I Learned

Training with MSE makes a model predict the average of all possible answers. That's why the pure-noise denoiser outputs the mean digit, and why one-step denoising is a bit blurry. Taking many small steps lets the model commit to one sharp answer instead.
